A public artwork made with old solar panels

SUNDIAL

Nine solar panels that have finished their working lives, hung on nine walls of a town. Each one tells the time with its shadow, keeps a record of its own sun, and once a year turns its day into a picture.

All nine see the same sun. None of them sees the same day. A wall facing the sea, a wall in the shade of the castle, an alley that loses the sun by three in the afternoon: each place writes itself into its dial. Same sun, different data.

Horas non numero nisi serenas. I count only the sunny hours.

One panel, three things
Every day, for everyone

A sundial

A rod on the panel casts a shadow across numbered hour marks. Anyone can read the solar time from it, with no phone and no electricity. Most people will only ever see this: a panel on a wall that tells the time.

All year, in the background

A sun monitor

Behind the panel, a small board powered by the panel itself writes down its sun every day. The record is collected on foot every two weeks and published here as Sole, the year-round sun record: a weekly page of 63 dials, nine panels by seven days.

One week a year

An event

For a week in summer, each panel draws its day live on your phone, through its own Wi-Fi, and seals it at sunset. You can leave a line in each panel's book. The week becomes one four-colour screen print.

How to read a dial

Solar noon at the top. Morning on the left, evening on the right.

  • Ochre: a quarter of an hour of direct sun on this panel.
  • Blue: a quarter of an hour of shade or cloud. Shade is a true record, not a missing one.
  • The red line: how much the panel warmed up since sunrise.
  • Dots: visitors in that hour. Small, medium or large; never who they were.
  • The black line: the horizon. The day is sealed at sunset and nothing after dark is counted.
  • An empty outline: a day the panel did not wake. It still counts.

Short winter days make shorter arcs; the longest day of the year fills the half circle.

How it works

No internet at the panels. Everything moves on foot.

The panels are not connected to anything. Visitors find them with the map on this website, then join each panel's own Wi-Fi to see its dial. The only way the panels' records reach this website is someone walking past with a phone to collect them.

The rules

Nine panels, never more, never less

A SUNDIAL is always nine panels in one town. Each panel runs only on its own sun: no battery charged from the grid, no cable, no internet. When the light comes up the panel wakes; after sunset it sleeps.

Visitors are counted, never identified. A phone is told apart only for an hour, and nothing about it is kept. The book keeps only what a visitor chooses to write.

SUNDIAL will be open source once it has been tested in its first town, so other towns can make their own nine.

A short history, mostly invented

Heliotropism

Heliotropism is the art of turning towards the sun. Its followers, the Heliotropists, say their first member was a sunflower that grew at the foot of an old wall by the sea, under a sundial so worn that only its motto could still be read. Someone who passed every day drew a mark behind it each quarter of an hour, one colour for sun and another for shade, closing each day at sunset. By the end of the summer the wall was covered in unsigned days, no two alike, all made by the same sun.

Every summer thousands of people turn south and follow the sun to towns like Bosa. By the Heliotropists' count, every one of them is a member.
